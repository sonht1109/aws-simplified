Introduction to AWS & the Cloud

What "the cloud" really is, how public and private clouds differ, why so many companies use AWS, how AWS spreads its data centers around the world, and how to open your own account.

Before the cloud: data centers

Ten to twenty years ago, if a company wanted to run an application, it bought physical servers (powerful computers built to run software 24/7) from vendors like IBM or HP. Those servers lived in a data center: a room or building full of servers, with:

  • lots of network cables and the company's own network setup,
  • cooling to keep the right temperature,
  • backup power so nothing switches off,
  • a team of people to patch, upgrade and guard everything around the clock.

There was also a big waste problem. One server might have 100 CPUs and 100 GB of RAM. If you ran a single small application that needed only 1 CPU and 1 GB, the other 99% sat idle. Need another app? Buy another server.

Virtualization: one server, many machines

Virtualization fixes that waste. A piece of software called a hypervisor runs on the physical server and splits it into several virtual machines (VMs). Each VM behaves like its own separate computer with its own operating system, CPU share, memory and disk.

So what is "the cloud"?

Once servers are virtualized, VMs can be created on request and handed out over the network. A developer asks for five servers, an admin creates five VMs and sends back their IP addresses. The developer uses them without knowing (or caring) which building or rack they physically live in.

That is the core idea of cloud computing: computing resources (servers, storage, databases and more) delivered on demand over a network, while the physical hardware stays somewhere out of sight, "in the cloud".

Private, public and hybrid cloud

Private cloud

A company builds this cloud setup inside its own data center, only for its own staff. Its own team buys, runs and secures everything, often with platforms like OpenStack or VMware. Banks and others with very sensitive data still do this.

Public cloud

A provider such as AWS, Microsoft Azure or Google Cloud owns huge data centers worldwide and rents resources to anyone with an account. You pay only for what you use ("pay as you go").

Hybrid cloud

A mix: some systems stay in the company's own data center (for example, a sensitive database), while others run on a public cloud. Common during a slow move to the cloud.

On-premises (or "on-prem") simply means "in our own building, on our own hardware", which is where private clouds live.

Think of it like…Owning a car vs. using a taxi app. Owning (private cloud) gives full control, but you pay for fuel, insurance, repairs and parking even when it sits unused. The taxi (public cloud) is there when you need it, someone else does the maintenance, and you pay per ride.
"Isn't public cloud less secure?"A common worry is that your servers share hardware with other companies. AWS keeps customers isolated from each other, and you can build your own private, walled-off network inside AWS. That is a VPC, covered in Lesson 6.

Why public cloud is so popular

Most people assume the main reason is cost. Cost matters, but the first reason is usually maintenance.

  1. No maintenance overhead. A 50-person startup would need several people just to run a data center: patching servers, fixing hardware, watching for power loss and attacks, 24/7. On a public cloud you create an account, give your team access, and start building.
  2. Cost and pay-as-you-go. No large upfront purchase. Turn servers off at night or during holidays and you stop paying for them. Providers buy hardware at enormous scale, so it's cheaper per unit.
  3. Speed and choice. Get a server in minutes instead of waiting weeks for hardware delivery. AWS started with a handful of offerings (virtual servers, storage) and now has more than 200 services (ready-made products such as databases or managed Kubernetes).

Is everyone leaving the cloud?

You may hear about cloud repatriation: companies moving from public cloud back to their own data centers, usually because of security rules or because they didn't get the savings they expected. It is real but small, a tiny percentage of companies. Startups and mid-size companies especially can't afford to build and staff their own data centers, so cloud adoption keeps growing.

Why AWS?

  • First mover. AWS pioneered public cloud. Many companies started their cloud journey on AWS over a decade ago and stayed.
  • Largest market share, followed by Azure and then Google Cloud. More companies on AWS means more AWS job openings.
  • Skills transfer. The concepts are the same across clouds; names, screens and some details differ. Learning AWS first is a safe bet.

Regions and Availability Zones

AWS's data centers are spread across the world, and you choose where your resources live.

  • A Region is a geographic area with AWS data centers, such as Mumbai (ap-south-1), Frankfurt (eu-central-1) or N. Virginia (us-east-1). Regions are independent of each other.
  • An Availability Zone (AZ) is one or more separate data centers inside a region, with its own power and networking. A region has several AZs, named with a letter: us-east-1a, us-east-1b, …

How to choose a region

  • Law and data rules. A European bank may require that its data never leaves Europe, so you'd pick Frankfurt or Ireland.
  • Latency (the delay for a request to travel to the server and back). Put servers close to your users. A US customer hitting a server in Mumbai will feel the delay.
  • Price and features. Prices differ a little between regions, and the newest services sometimes appear in a few regions first.

Why AZs exist: a fire, flood or power cut can take down a data center. If your app runs in only one AZ, it goes down too. Spreading copies across AZs gives you high availability (staying up even when parts fail).

TipMost AWS resources belong to one region, and the console shows one region at a time (selector in the top-right). If a server you created "disappeared", check that you are looking at the right region. While learning, pick one region near you and keep everything there. Some newer regions are "opt-in" and must be enabled before use.

Who is responsible for what?

Moving to the cloud doesn't hand over all the work. AWS calls this the shared responsibility model:

AWS secures the cloud

Buildings, hardware, power, cooling, the physical network and the virtualization layer.

You secure what's in the cloud

Who can log in, your data, firewall rules, your server's operating system updates and your application.

Create your AWS account

You'll use one account for the whole course. The sign-up screens change from time to time, but the steps are roughly:

  1. Go to the AWS website and choose Create an AWS account.
  2. Enter an email address and an account name. This email becomes the root user, the account owner with unlimited power (more in Lesson 3). A fresh email just for AWS is a good idea. The account name can be changed later.
  3. Verify the email with the code AWS sends you, then set a strong password.
  4. Choose Personal (for learning), then enter your name, phone number and address. The address is used for billing and tax.
  5. Add a debit or credit card. AWS makes a tiny temporary charge (around 1 USD or the local equivalent) to confirm the card is real, and releases it. This stops people from creating spam accounts. Not every card is accepted; use a proper card from a bank rather than "virtual card" workarounds.
  6. Some countries ask for tax details (for example a PAN in India). Then verify your phone and pick the free Basic support plan.
Watch out: the free tier has limitsAWS's free tier lets you learn at little or no cost, but only within limits (for example, a set number of hours of a small server per month, or a pool of credits on newer accounts). Go beyond them and the charges are billed to your card. Always pick resources marked "Free tier eligible", delete what you don't need, and set a budget alert (see Lesson 36). Free tier terms change, so check AWS's current Free Tier page.

A quick console tour

The AWS Management Console is the website where you click to create and manage resources.

  • Search bar (top): type a service name like "EC2" or "IAM" to jump straight to it.
  • Region selector (top right): the region you're currently working in.
  • Account menu (top right): account ID, billing, security credentials, sign out.
  • Recently visited and Favorites: shortcuts to services you use often.

Clicking around isn't the only way in; later you'll also drive AWS from the terminal with the AWS CLI.

Check yourself

What is the main difference between a private and a public cloud?
Both use virtualization. The difference is who owns and runs the data centers, and who is allowed to request resources.
Your app runs on one server in us-east-1a. That AZ loses power. What happens, and what's the fix?
AZs are isolated so one failure doesn't take down the others, but only if your app actually runs in more than one AZ.
According to the lesson, what's usually the first reason startups choose public cloud?
Cost matters, but avoiding the 24/7 work of buying, patching, cooling and guarding servers is the bigger win.

Key takeaways

  • Virtualization splits one physical server into many VMs; the cloud delivers such resources on demand over a network.
  • Private cloud = your own data center for your own staff; public cloud = a provider rents resources to anyone; hybrid = a mix.
  • Public cloud wins mainly by removing maintenance work, then by pay-as-you-go cost and speed.
  • AWS leads the market (first mover), so it has the most jobs, and the concepts transfer to other clouds.
  • Regions are geographic areas; AZs are isolated data centers inside a region. Pick regions for law and latency, and use several AZs for high availability.
  • Stay inside free-tier limits and clean up: overage charges go to your card.
